When you ask a yes/no research question, the Consensus Meter reads the top papers in your results and shows how many of them say **yes**, **no**, or **possibly**. It is a fast way to gauge where the evidence stands before you open any single paper.

## When the Consensus Meter appears

The meter appears when your query is phrased as a yes/no question, for example "Does creatine improve strength?" or "Is intermittent fasting effective for weight loss?". Consensus reads the stance of each of the top papers and aggregates those stances into a single view.

If your question is open-ended (for example "What are the effects of creatine on strength?"), there is no yes/no stance to aggregate, so you will not see a meter. Rephrasing an open-ended query as a yes/no question is the fastest way to bring it up.

## How to read it

<Steps>
  <Step title="Check the overall split">
    The meter summarizes how the top papers divide across **yes**, **no**, and **possibly**. A strong lean in one direction suggests the literature broadly agrees. An even split suggests the question is contested or still unsettled.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Read the possibly bucket carefully">
    **Possibly** covers papers with a qualified, partial, or conditional answer. These often hold the most useful nuance: effects that depend on dose, population, or study design.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Open the papers behind the split">
    The meter is a starting point, not a conclusion. Open the papers on each side to see the study designs, sample sizes, and findings driving the result. The [Study Snapshot](/web/study-snapshot) helps you judge each one quickly.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
  The Consensus Meter is a heuristic snapshot, not a formal meta-analysis. It reflects the stances of the top papers Consensus retrieved for your query, not a systematic review of the entire literature. Treat it as a quick read on the state of the evidence, and verify the underlying papers before you rely on the result for anything high-stakes.
</Warning>
